"What will an ETP cost?" is the first question almost every plant owner asks, and the honest answer is: it depends on your wastewater. Two plants with the same capacity in KLD can differ in price several times over because one treats mild washing water and the other treats high-COD chemical effluent. This article explains what actually drives the cost of an Effluent Treatment Plant (ETP) in India, what running costs to plan for, and how to get a quote you can rely on.

1. The seven factors that decide ETP cost

FactorWhy it matters
Flow (KLD)Sets the size of every tank, pump and blower. Peak and batch discharges matter as much as the daily average.
Pollution loadHigh COD/BOD, oil, colour, heavy metals or toxic compounds need more treatment stages and longer retention.
TDS (dissolved salts)Conventional treatment does not remove salts. High TDS may require RO and evaporation, which changes the budget completely.
Required outlet qualityDischarge to a sewer or CETP, discharge to land or surface water, reuse, or ZLD — each step up costs more.
Technology choicePhysico-chemical, biological (ASP, MBBR, SBR, MBR), advanced oxidation, membranes and evaporators all have different capital and running costs.
Civil or prefabricatedRCC tanks, MS/FRP tanks or skid-mounted packages; site soil, space and levels also affect civil cost.
Automation and sludge handlingPLC controls, online meters, filter press or screw press add cost but reduce manpower and errors.

2. Indicative budgets

As a broad guide, small ETPs of around 10–50 KLD for moderate effluents often start in the range of ₹5–15 lakh. Larger industrial plants, high-strength effluents and especially systems with RO and evaporators (ZLD) can cost many times more. These figures are only for orientation; a responsible quote is always based on your own effluent data.

Be careful with "per KLD" pricing

A price quoted only per KLD, without any wastewater analysis, usually means a standard design that ignores your actual load. That is the most common reason new plants fail to meet MPCB norms.

3. Running cost: the part buyers forget

Over the life of a plant, operating cost often exceeds the purchase price. Plan for:

  • Power: aeration blowers and pumps running round the clock; usually the largest item.
  • Chemicals: pH correction, coagulants, flocculants, nutrients, disinfectants and, for RO, antiscalants and cleaning chemicals.
  • Manpower: trained operators across shifts, or an O&M contract.
  • Sludge disposal: chemical ETP sludge is usually hazardous waste and must go to an authorised facility; see our hazardous waste management service.
  • Testing and compliance: periodic laboratory analysis and annual returns.
  • Maintenance and spares: diffusers, pumps, media, membranes and filter cloths.

4. How to get a quote you can trust

  1. Measure the flow over several days, including peak and batch discharges.
  2. Analyse representative samples for pH, COD, BOD, TSS, TDS, oil and grease and industry-specific parameters.
  3. Check your consent for the outlet limits and any ZLD or reuse condition.
  4. Run a treatability study for difficult effluents, to prove the process before you build.
  5. Compare proposals on the same basis: design basis, process flow diagram, equipment make and capacity, civil scope, guarantees and running cost estimate.

5. Five mistakes that make an ETP expensive later

  • No equalisation tank, so shock loads upset the biological stage.
  • Ignoring TDS until the plant is built and the outlet still fails.
  • Undersized sludge handling, leading to sludge carry-over and poor outlet quality.
  • No provision for future production growth.
  • Choosing the lowest capital price with high power and chemical consumption.

6. Upgrade instead of replace?

If you already have an ETP that struggles, replacing it is not always necessary. A performance audit often shows that converting the aeration tank to MBBR, improving equalisation or adding tertiary filtration brings the plant within norms at a fraction of the cost of a new one.
