Receiving a notice from the Maharashtra Pollution Control Board (MPCB) is stressful, especially when the words "closure" or "disconnection of electricity and water supply" appear. The good news is that most notices can be resolved if you respond quickly, honestly and with evidence. The bad news is that ignoring a notice, or replying with a one-line letter, is the fastest way to turn a show-cause notice into a closure order. This article explains the common types of notices and a practical plan for the first seven days.

Note

This article is general guidance, not legal advice. Every notice has its own facts and deadlines; read yours carefully and take professional advice for serious matters.

1. Types of notices you may receive

NoticeWhat it usually means
Show-cause noticeThe board has found a violation (for example during an inspection or from monitoring data) and asks why action should not be taken.
Proposed directionsThe board intends to issue directions and gives you an opportunity to respond or be heard first.
Interim / final directionsSpecific actions you must take, sometimes with a bank guarantee to secure compliance.
Closure directionsSerious or repeated non-compliance; may include disconnection of power and water. Issued under the powers given to boards by the Water and Air Acts.

2. Why notices are issued

  • Treated effluent or stack emissions exceeding consent limits.
  • Operating without valid consent, or beyond the consented capacity or products.
  • ETP/STP not operated, bypassed or inadequate.
  • Exceedances or data gaps on online monitoring systems.
  • Improper storage or disposal of hazardous waste, or missing manifests.
  • Annual returns or the Environmental Statement not filed.
  • Public complaints about odour, smoke, noise or discharge.

3. Your first seven days: a practical plan

  1. Day 1 — Read it line by line. Note the exact violations alleged, the legal provisions cited, the reply deadline and any hearing date. Put the deadline in everyone's calendar.
  2. Day 1–2 — Collect your documents. Consent order, recent test reports, logbooks, hazardous waste records, returns filed, inspection report (if shared) and photographs.
  3. Day 2–3 — Find the root cause. Was it a one-off event or a systemic problem — an undersized ETP, a broken blower, an expansion without amendment? An honest diagnosis is the foundation of a credible reply.
  4. Day 3–5 — Act immediately on what you can fix. Repair equipment, stop any bypass, clear old hazardous waste through authorised channels, file pending returns. Document every action with dates and photographs.
  5. Day 4–5 — Get independent evidence. Fresh effluent and stack testing through an accredited laboratory shows the current status.
  6. Day 5–6 — Prepare a time-bound action plan. For problems that need more time (for example a plant upgrade), give a realistic schedule with milestones and interim measures.
  7. Day 6–7 — Submit a complete reply before the deadline, with annexures, and request a personal hearing if appropriate.

4. What a strong reply contains

  • A point-by-point response to each observation in the notice.
  • Facts and evidence, not general assurances.
  • Corrective actions already completed, with proof.
  • A time-bound action plan for the remaining items.
  • Commitment to ongoing monitoring and reporting.

A short letter saying "we will comply" without evidence rarely works and can harm your case.

5. If closure directions have already been issued

Closure can be revoked once the board is satisfied that the causes have been addressed. In practice this means completing the corrective work, documenting it thoroughly, applying for revocation and often furnishing a bank guarantee as required. The Water and Air Acts also provide appeal routes against orders; take legal advice on whether an appeal is appropriate in your case.

6. How to avoid notices in the first place

  • Operate treatment systems every day and keep logbooks up to date.
  • Monitor at the frequency your consent requires and act on any failing result.
  • Amend your consent before expansions or product changes.
  • File returns and the Environmental Statement on time; see our guide to Form V.
  • Run a periodic third-party compliance audit so gaps are found internally, not by an inspector.
